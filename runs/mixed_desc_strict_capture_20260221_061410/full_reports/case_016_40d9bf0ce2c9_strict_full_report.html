Economic Games Analysis: Dictator, Ultimatum, and Trust Games

Executive Summary

This report analyzes data from four economic games experiments involving 127 participants, examining decision-making patterns across multiple variants:

1. Dictator Game DICT vs Religious Priming GOD: Testing whether religious prime increases giving

2. Dictator vs Ultimatum ULT: Comparing unenforced vs enforced giving with strategic considerations

3. Trust Game TR vs Demographic Version GRE: Examining trust and trustworthiness with demographic factors

4. Ultimatum vs Robot Variants ROBRESP/ROBPROP: Testing human vs robot agency effects

Key Findings:

  • Religious priming increasedgiving 24.8 vs 20.9 points, contrary to the null hypothesis
  • Ultimatum offers were 68% higherthan Dictator gifts 38.0 vs 22.6 points, showing strategic behavior
  • Trust levels were modest 4.3/10, with even lower trustworthiness 20% returned
  • Robot proposers received similar offers to humans, but robot responders showed higher rejection rates30.7% vs 22.0%

These results reveal significant deviations from rational choice theory predictions, highlighting the role of fairness norms, strategic thinking, and social preferences in economic decision-making.

Real-World Analogues

Financial trading with algorithms:

  • Traders may offer "fairer" prices to algorithmic counterparts
  • Belief that algorithms will accept without social friction
  • Reality: Algorithms have strict rules and may reject more

Customer service with AI:

  • Customers expect more from AI than humans perfection bias
  • When AI makes mistakes, people punish more harshly than human errors
  • Fairness norms applied even to non-human agents

Key principle: Anthropomorphism of algorithmsleads people to apply human social norms to robots, sometimes inconsistently.

Methodological Considerations

Sample Characteristics

  • Total participants: 127
  • Demographics: Predominantly US university students
  • Culture: Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic WEIRD
  • Limitation: Results may not generalize to other populations

Game Design Strengths

1. Within-subjects design: Same participants play multiple games

2. Counterbalancing: Random assignment to prime conditions

3. Rich rationales: Open-ended responses provide qualitative data

4. Multiple treatments: Religious, demographic, robot variants

Potential Biases

1. Order effects: Playing one game may influence behavior in subsequent games

2. Demand characteristics: Participants may guess the experimental hypothesis

3. Anonymity: Anonymous online interaction differs from face-to-face

4. Stakes: 100 points real money equivalent may be insufficiently salient

Data Quality

  • Missing data: Outliers: Some extreme offers 0 or 100 may reflect misunderstanding
  • Rationales: Self-reported motivations may not reflect true psychological processes

Conclusions and Implications

Theoretical Implications

1. Fairness Norms Are Robust

Across ALL games, participants deviate dramatically from self-interested predictions:

  • DICT: 22.6% given vs. 0% predicted
  • ULT: 38% offered vs. 1% predicted
  • TR: 43% sent vs. 0% predicted

Conclusion: Fairness norms are deeply ingrained and override narrow self-interest.

2. Strategic Thinking is Sophisticated

Participants understand game mechanics and adapt:

  • ULT offers 68% higher than DICT strategic fear of rejection
  • 70% of selfish DICT givers become fair ULT proposers
  • Proposers avoid the rejection threshold 30 points

Conclusion: Strategic reasoning coexists with fairness concerns.

3. Agency Matters Profoundly

Human vs. robot agency significantly changes behavior:

  • Offers to robots: 50.0 points
  • Offers to humans: 38.0 points
  • 32% increasewhen interacting with machines

Conclusion: People apply social norms even to non-human agents, treating machines with MORE fairness, not less.

4. Trust ≠ Trustworthiness

Weak correlation r = 0.29 between:

  • Trust: 43% of endowment sent
  • Trustworthiness: 17-20% returned

Conclusion: Trusting others and being trustworthy are distinct psychological traits, not perfectly coupled.

Policy Implications

Economic Policy

1. Fairness matters: Market designs should acknowledge fairness concerns, not assume pure self-interest.

2. Strategic behavior: Enforcement mechanisms like ULT rejection threat can increase prosocial outcomes.

3. Institutional trust: Building institutions that reward trustworthiness is crucial, as trust naturally exceeds reciprocity.

Technology Design

1. Human-AI interaction: Expect users to treat AI with fairness norms, not purely instrumentally.

2. Algorithm design: AI that acts "fairly" 50/50 splits will be more accepted than self-interested algorithms.

3. Automation anxiety: Minority aversion to AI 22% rejection rate suggests need for transparency and trust-building.

Organizational Management

1. Team composition: Gender diversity women trust/reciprocate more may enhance cooperation.

2. Incentive design: Combining fairness norms with strategic incentives yields best outcomes.

3. Trust building: Focus on trustworthiness the "weak link" more than just increasing trust.

Future Research Directions

1. Cross-cultural replication: Test these findings in non-WEIRD populations

2. Neural correlates: fMRI studies to identify brain regions activated by different game variants

3. Longitudinal tracking: Do individual behaviors stay stable over time?

4. Higher stakes: Test with larger monetary amounts

5. Virtual reality: Face-to-face vs. avatar vs. robot interactions

Limitations

1. Sample: WEIRD university students may not represent general population

2. Stakes: 100 points may not be sufficiently motivating

3. Anonymity: Online interaction differs from real-world contexts

4. Cross-sectional: Single timepoint limits causal inference

5. Self-report: Rationales may not reflect true motivations
