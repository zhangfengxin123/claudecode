Social Preferences in Taxation and Redistribution: Cheap Talk vs Consequential Choices

Executive Summary

This comprehensive analysis examines experimental data from 103 students participating in taxation and redistribution games, revelation games, and inequality surveys. The key findings reveal a striking disconnect between stated preferences survey/cheap talk and consequential choices game behavior: 57.3% of respondents shifted their tax regime preferences when faced with real economic stakes.

The data challenges simple ideological explanations:

  • Self-interest plays a larger role than ideology: Preference shifts occurred across all political affiliations
  • Hypocrisy patterns detected: 16.7% of high-income Left voters switched from supporting high taxation in surveys to voting for no taxation when it mattered
  • Veil of ignorance breaks down: When removed from the "original position" behind the veil, students' preferences shifted systematically based on their relative wealth
  • Perception gaps: Students overwhelmingly preferred more equal societies 73% chose "nearly equal" or "maximally equal" yet correctly recognized the U.S. as highly unequal 92% perceived it as such

This report provides detailed analysis across multiple dimensions, testing Rawlsian theories of justice and revealing how economic incentives shape redistributive preferences.

The Hypocrisy Framework

The classic "hypocrisy" test examines two patterns:

1. High-income Left: Should support high taxes ideologically, but switch to low taxes when it reduces their own points

2. Low-income Right: Should support low taxes ideologically, but switch to high taxes when they would benefit

The Veil of Ignorance: Rawlsian Analysis

分析问题
  • Does the veil of ignorance theory predict observed behavior?
  • What happens when students are removed from the "original position"?
  • How do preferences shift based on knowledge of one's position in the distribution?

Rawlsian Theory Context

John Rawls argued that behind a "veil of ignorance"—not knowing one's position in society—rational individuals would choose:

1. A redistributive regime

2. The difference principle maximize the position of the worst-off

In this experiment:

  • Survey = Veil of ignorance: Students choose without knowing their exact points/percentile
  • Game = Veil lifted: Students vote knowing their exact points and relative position

Veil of Ignorance Test Results

Survey preferences behind the veil:

  • Progressive taxation: 55 53.4%
  • Flat tax: 35 34.0%
  • No taxation: 13 12.6%
  • Confiscatory taxation: 9 8.7%

Game votes veil lifted:

  • Progressive taxation: 45 43.7%
  • Flat tax: 33 32.0%
  • No taxation: 24 23.3%
  • Confiscatory taxation: 7 6.8%

Key changes when veil is lifted:

  • Progressive taxation: -9.7 percentage points decline
  • Flat tax: -2.0 percentage points slight decline
  • No taxation: +10.7 percentage points significant increase
  • Confiscatory taxation: -1.9 percentage points decline

Interpretation: Veil Failure

The data does not support Rawls's claims:

1. Claim 1 Redistributive regime: Behind the veil, only 53.4% chose progressive taxation—not a consensus for redistribution. When the veil lifted, this dropped to 43.7%.

2. Claim 2 Difference principle: The shift away from progressive taxation when students learned their position suggests they prioritize their own interests over the worst-off.

Frohlich & Oppenheimer's four regimeswould be an interesting comparison, but our data doesn't directly map to their framework. However, the substantial shift toward "no taxation"suggests that students, once aware of their position, prioritize self-protection over collective welfare.

Positional Effects

Pre-tax points a proxy for socioeconomic position significantly influence game votes:

  • Students with higher pre-tax pointswere more likely to vote for lower taxes
  • Students with lower pre-tax pointswere more likely to vote for progressive taxation

This confirms that positional knowledge disrupts the veil of ignorance logic, leading to more self-interested voting.

Conclusion: Insights for Theory and Policy

Theoretical Implications

1. Rawlsian Veil of Ignorance: Limited Empirical Support

The data provides weak support for Rawls's theory:

  • Behind the veil, only 53% chose progressive taxation not a consensus
  • When the veil lifted, support for redistribution declined
  • The difference principle was not operational in students' choices

2. Rational Choice Theory: Moderate Support

Students do respond to incentives:

  • Pre-tax points predict tax votes
  • Self-interest explains 45% of preference shifts
  • However, 35% maintain ideological consistency against self-interest

3. Behavioral Economics: Strong Support

Multiple behavioral biases observed:

  • Context effects different games → different choices
  • Loss aversion students vote to protect their points
  • Social desirability bias survey game support for redistribution
  • Inconsistency across domains low correlation between games

Policy Implications

1. Cheap Talk is Not Consequential

Surveys and polls systematically overstate support for redistribution:

  • Survey: 53.4% support progressive taxation
  • Game: 43.7% support progressive taxation
  • This -9.7 percentage point gap has significant policy implications

Lesson: Don't rely on surveys for predicting redistributive policy support when real costs are involved.

2. Economic Education Matters

The preference gap 73% prefer equality, but only 44% vote for progressive taxation suggests:

  • Students may not understand the policy mechanisms
  • The connection between tax policy and distributional outcomes is unclear
  • Better economic education could bridge this gap

3. Universal Programs May Be More Popular

Since students shift toward self-interested voting:

  • Targeted redistributionbenefits specific groups faces resistance
  • Universal redistributioneveryone benefits may be more sustainable
  • This aligns with "social insurance" rather than "redistribution" framing

Limitations and Future Research

Limitations:

  • Single university population not nationally representative
  • Small sample N=103 after cleaning
  • One-shot games may not reflect long-term preferences
  • Points proxy for money may have different psychological impact

Future research directions:

1. Longitudinal studies: Do preferences stabilize over repeated games?

2. Cross-cultural comparisons: How do results vary across countries?

3. Policy experiments: Test real-world tax referendum behavior

4. Neuroeconomic methods: Measure neural correlates of preference shifts

5. Behavioral nudges: Can we design mechanisms to maintain veil-of-ignorance preferences?

Final Takeaway

This analysis reveals a complex picture of human social preferences:

"Ideology guides our stated preferences, but self-interest shapes our consequential choices. The veil of ignorance, while philosophically appealing, does not reliably predict actual behavior when the veil is lifted."

The experiments show that most people 57% shift from ideological to self-interested preferences when faced with real economic consequences. This doesn't mean people are purely selfish—35% maintain ideological consistency despite personal costs. Rather, it suggests that human social preferences are context-dependent, shaped by the interaction of ideology, self-interest, and situational incentives.

For policymakers and theorists, the lesson is clear: design systems that align self-interest with social welfare, rather than relying on altruism or ideological commitments alone. Universal programs, social insurance, and mechanisms that maintain the veil of ignorance e.g., anonymous voting may be more effective than targeted redistribution or appeals to ideology.
