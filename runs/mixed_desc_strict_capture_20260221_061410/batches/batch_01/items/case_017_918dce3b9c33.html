Taxation and Redistribution Game

Students choose one of four tax/redistribution regimes (vote is consequential; taxes apply “for real”).

Compare survey preferences (cheap talk) vs game preferences (consequential):

Did preferences shift? In what direction, and why?

Explain how preferences (survey and game) vary with:

Parental household income, gender, party identification.

“Pocketbook vs ideology” analysis:

Pocketbook interest = pre-tax income (low vs high cumulative gaming points).

Ideology = party ID (Democrat, Ind. Leaning Liberal, Ind. Leaning Conservative, Republican).

Decide whether/how to aggregate party categories (e.g., Left = Dem + Ind-Lib; Right = Rep + Ind-Con).

Test “hypocrisy” patterns:

High-income Left: switch from high tax → low tax?

Low-income Right: switch from low tax → high tax?

Pick examples of switchers (“hypocrites”) and non-switchers, and compare their rationales.

Optional but useful: build a table counting stayed vs shifted, broken down by pre-tax income and party ID.

Veil of Ignorance / Rawls / Frohlich & Oppenheimer (1993)

In the survey, everyone is in the same “original position”; in the game, students differ by points/percentile.

Relate shifts to the veil of ignorance argument.

Rawls claims that behind the veil people will agree on:

a redistribution regime, and

the difference principle (raise the poorest before anyone richer gains).

Frohlich & Oppenheimer test this with four regimes:

Floor constraint (floor, no ceiling)

Maximum income (everyone keeps earnings; no floor/ceiling)

Range constraint (inequality allowed but capped gap)

Difference principle

Their subjects: US, Canada, Poland (Poland late-1980s communist; continuum from individualist → collectivist).

Use their regime-choice graphs + your class data to evaluate Rawls’s two claims.

Survey on Income Inequality (Norton & Ariely 2011 link)

Analyze inequality perceptions/preferences using Norton & Ariely as framing:

Norton & Ariely: wealth inequality; countries: fictional equal + Sweden + US.

Your survey: income inequality; countries: fictional equal + fictional unequal + Sweden, US, Colombia.

Connect income-inequality survey results to taxation-game choices.

Use the combined dataset: Taxation & Redistribution + Income Inequality.

Revelation Game (Lohmann & Verweij)


Combines Dictator (give) and Taking (you didn’t play taking game separately).


Identify:


Egoistical choice in Dictator: give 0 / keep all.


Determine egoistical choice in Revelation Game and interpret 0 (neither give nor take) using rationales (don’t guess).




Key mechanic: giving is doubled, taking is halved:


Giving becomes a socially efficient public good.


Taking becomes a socially inefficient public bad.




“Correlate” behavior across games (no need for coefficients):


Dictator vs Revelation giving


Public Goods vs Revelation giving


Does efficiency increase giving vs Dictator?




Players observe both players’ cumulative points at decision time:


Do they take from richer / give to poorer?


Examine odd cases (give to richer, take from poorer) via rationales.




Classify into 5 types and describe distribution:


Give-Alls, 2) Give-Somes, 3) Neither-Nors, 4) Take-Somes, 5) Take-Alls.




Rationale-based deep dive (labor intensive):


Sample ~25 students (e.g., 5 per subgroup or proportional), randomly (use Random Row Picker).




Explore predictors: gender, parental income, rich/poor, econ major, law school, religious practice, spirituality/religiosity, volunteer hours/orgs, left-right spectrum, party.


Identify normative ethics arguments in rationales: utilitarianism, rights, justice/fairness, virtue ethics.


Compare with Taxation Game: both allow “game point theft”


Taxation: collective/class-wide


Revelation: individual/pairwise


Compare types (steal in both vs neither, etc.).
