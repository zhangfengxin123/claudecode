Semi-Autonomous Mathematics Discovery with Gemini: A Case Study Analysis

Executive Summary

This analysis examines a groundbreaking case study on using AI specifically Google's Gemini Deep Think to solve open mathematical problems from Paul Erdős's extensive catalog. The study, conducted in December 2025, involved analyzing 700 problems marked as "Open" in Bloom's Erdős Problems database. The results reveal that many so-called "unsolved" problems were actually already resolved in the literature but remained obscure—highlighting a fundamental issue in mathematical knowledge organization rather than inherent difficulty.

Key Findings:

  • 13 problems successfully addressedthrough a hybrid AI-human workflow
  • Only 6.5% of AI responseswere meaningfully correct, with 68.5% fundamentally flawed
  • Most "open" problems were obscure, not difficult—already solved but not indexed
  • Success rate: 1.86%13/700 of open problems resolved
  • Literature identification emerged as the most challenging step

1.1 Project Scope

The study deployed a custom mathematics research agent called Aletheiabuilt on Gemini Deep Think on December 2-9, 2025, targeting 700 Erdős problems marked as "Open" in Thomas Bloom's database ErdosProblems.com. At the time of the study, the database tracked 1,179 problems total, with 483 41% classified as solved, leaving 700 problems as potentially open challenges.

1.2 The Aletheia Approach

Aletheia employed a sophisticated verifier mechanismthat used natural language verification to narrow the search space. This initial filtering proved crucial:

  • Input: 700 "Open" problem prompts
  • AI responses marked as potentially correct: 212 30.3%
  • After human mathematician filtering: 27 solution candidates
  • Final verified solutions: 13 problems

The two-stage human evaluation process was designed for efficiency:

1. First pass: Generalist mathematicians quickly eliminated clearly incorrect solutions

2. Second pass: Domain experts carefully vetted remaining candidates, consulting external experts when correctness was established but novelty was uncertain

These demonstrate AI's capability to handle complex, multi-faceted mathematical problems even when complete solutions remain elusive.

While all credit belongs to the original authors, these cases are distinguished from pure literature identification because they suggest stronger implications for AI capabilities—the AI independently derived correct mathematical proofs without access to the existing literature.

3.2 The "Trivial Solution" Problem

A revealing finding: 50 of the 63 technically correct solutions79.4% were mathematically meaningless because the problem statements were interpreted in ways that didn't capture Erdős's intent, leading to trivial solutions.

For example, if a problem asked to prove a bound holds "for all sufficiently large n," an AI might produce a technically correct proof that the bound holds when "all n = 1" trivial interpretation rather than the intended asymptotic behavior.

This highlights a fundamental challenge: mathematical intent and contextare crucial, and current AI systems struggle to capture the nuance and spirit of mathematical problems as intended by their proposers.

3.3 Literature Verification: The Hardest Step

Surprisingly, the lengthiest and most arduous stepwas investigating whether solutions were already in the literature and whether they truly addressed the intended problem. This process required:

  • Searching through decades of mathematical literature
  • Understanding specialized terminology across multiple fields
  • Determining whether solutions were novel or known results
  • Verifying that solutions addressed the intended problem, not trivial interpretations

The difficulty of this step suggests that mathematical knowledge organizationremains a significant bottleneck, and that AI's ability to navigate the vast, fragmented mathematical literature is still limited.

5. Key Insights and Implications

分析问题
  • What is the main conclusion about the "open" status of Erdős problems?
  • What does this study suggest about AI's current capabilities in mathematics?

5.1 The Obscurity Thesis

Primary Finding: The "Open" status of many Erdős problems was due to obscurity rather than difficulty. Many problems marked as unsolved had already been resolved in the literature but were not easily discoverable through internet searches.

This finding has profound implications:

1. Database Reliability: Mathematical problem databases may not accurately reflect the true state of knowledge

2. Knowledge Fragmentation: Mathematical results are scattered across numerous journals, preprints, conference proceedings, and even blog posts

3. Indexing Gap: Current search and indexing methods fail to capture much of mathematical knowledge

4. Rediscovery Waste: Researchers may waste time rediscovering known results

The study cites an OpenAI announcement from October 2025 where GPT-5 identified ten "Open" problems that had already been solved—providing independent confirmation of this phenomenon.

5.2 AI Capabilities Assessment

The study provides a nuanced assessment of AI's current mathematical capabilities:

Strengths:

  • Can find correct solutions to some nontrivial mathematical problems
  • Effective at reducing problems to existing literature
  • Can generate technically valid mathematical arguments
  • Useful for exploring problem spaces and generating candidate solutions

Limitations:

  • Low success rate: Only 6.5% of responses are meaningfully correct
  • Interpretation challenges: Struggles with mathematical intent and context
  • Error-prone: Frequently contains mathematical errors constants, citations, inequalities
  • Literature discovery: Cannot reliably determine novelty or identify existing solutions
  • Trivial solutions: Often misinterprets problems to generate trivial answers

5.3 Subconscious Plagiarism Risk

The study raises an important concern: AI systems risk "subconscious plagiarism"by reproducing results from the training data without proper attribution or awareness that the result already exists. This presents challenges for:

  • Academic integrity: Determining novelty when AI systems have access to vast training data
  • Intellectual property: Respecting existing work and proper attribution
  • Research validation: Ensuring AI-generated results are genuinely novel

The authors recommend transparency about AI's potential access to prior work and thorough literature verification before claiming novelty.

5.4 Benchmarking Value

Despite limitations, the study concludes that Erdős problems provide an engaging new benchmark for AI research:

  • Well-defined problems: Clear success criteria
  • Varied difficulty: Range from elementary to highly challenging
  • Diverse domains: Spans multiple areas of mathematics
  • Historical significance: Connection to one of mathematics' most prolific problem-solvers

However, the authors caution against overexcitement about mathematical significance—the problems solved were low-hanging fruit, not major breakthroughs.

6. Challenges and Future Directions

6.1 Identified Challenges

The study identifies several critical challenges in applying AI to mathematics at scale:

6.1.1 Literature Identification

Problem: AI cannot reliably determine if a solution is novel or already exists in the literature.

Root Causes:

  • Fragmented mathematical literature across journals, preprints, conference proceedings
  • Inconsistent terminology and notation across subfields
  • Results in non-indexed sources blog posts, technical reports, PhD theses
  • Language barriers results published in various languages

Impact: This was identified as the most time-consuming and challenging stepin the entire process.

6.1.2 Mathematical Intent Understanding

Problem: AI often misinterprets problem statements, leading to technically correct but trivial solutions.

Examples:

  • Interpreting "for all sufficiently large n" as "for n = 1"
  • Solving a degenerate case rather than the general problem
  • Ignoring implicit constraints or conventions in a field

Impact: 79.4% of technically correct solutions were mathematically meaningless due to interpretation issues.

6.1.3 Accuracy and Reliability

Problem: AI-generated mathematical proofs contain errors.

Documented Issues:

  • Incorrect constants e.g., citing exponents 2/3, 2/3 instead of 3/5, 4/5
  • Non-existent citations e.g., citing "Theorem 2.1 from Pach-Sharir 1992" that doesn't exist
  • Strict vs. non-strict inequality errors
  • Dependency issues in limit arguments

Impact: 68.5% of AI responses were fundamentally flawed.

6.2 Potential Improvements

Based on the findings, several directions for improving AI mathematical capabilities emerge:

6.2.1 Better Literature Access

Approaches:

  • Integrate formal mathematical databases and theorem provers
  • Develop specialized mathematical search engines
  • Train on structured mathematical representations not just natural language
  • Implement citation verification systems

6.2.2 Intent Recognition

Approaches:

  • Train on curated datasets with explicit problem intent annotations
  • Incorporate examples of trivial vs. nontrivial solutions
  • Use few-shot learning with expert-provided examples
  • Implement "spirit of the problem" detection

6.2.3 Formal Verification

Approaches:

  • Integrate with proof assistants Lean, Coq, Isabelle
  • Automatically formalize AI-generated proofs
  • Use proof checking to catch errors
  • Leverage formal mathematics libraries for verification

The study notes that Erdős-1051 was formalized in Lean 4, and this process helped identify and correct errors in the AI's original output.

6.2.4 Human-in-the-Loop Systems

Approaches:

  • Design workflows that maximize human expertise efficiency
  • Develop tools for rapid human filtering of AI outputs
  • Create collaborative interfaces for human-AI mathematical work
  • Implement progressive verification systems

The study's two-stage human evaluation process generalists first, domain experts second proved effective and could be optimized further.

6.3 Future Research Directions

The study suggests several promising research directions:

1. Automated Literature Discovery: Develop systems to reliably identify existing solutions in the mathematical literature

2. Mathematical Knowledge Graphs: Create structured representations of mathematical knowledge to improve navigation and discovery

3. Benchmark Development: Expand beyond Erdős problems to include more challenging and diverse mathematical benchmarks

4. Specialized Mathematical AI: Develop AI systems specifically trained and optimized for mathematical reasoning

5. Collaborative Platforms: Build better tools for human-AI collaboration in mathematical research

6. Novelty Detection: Create methods to assess whether AI-generated solutions are genuinely new

7. Intent Understanding: Improve AI's ability to capture the spirit and context of mathematical problems, not just their literal statements

7. Conclusions

分析问题
  • What is the primary conclusion of this study?
  • Should we be excited or cautious about AI in mathematics?
  • What are the practical takeaways for researchers?

7.1 Primary Conclusion

The study reaches a measured conclusion: there is low-hanging fruit among the Erdős problems, and AI has progressed to be capable of harvesting some of it. However, the authors emphasize caution against overexcitement about the mathematical significance of these results.

The 13 problems addressed were not major breakthroughsbut rather problems that could be solved through:

  • Straightforward applications of existing theorems
  • Reduction to known techniques
  • Construction of specific examples
  • Partial progress on multi-part problems

7.2 A Balanced Assessment

Reasons for Optimism:

  • AI can genuinely solve nontrivial mathematical problems
  • Hybrid human-AI workflows show promise for accelerating mathematical discovery
  • Erdős problems provide a valuable new AI benchmark
  • AI can serve as a tool for exploring problem spaces and generating candidate solutions

Reasons for Caution:

  • Success rate remains low 1.86% overall, 6.5% of meaningful responses
  • Most "open" problems solved were actually already known obscurity, not difficulty
  • AI struggles with mathematical intent and context
  • Literature identification is a major unsolved challenge
  • Risk of subconscious plagiarism and attribution issues

7.3 Practical Recommendations

For AI Researchers:

  • Focus on improving literature access and novelty detection
  • Develop better intent understanding capabilities
  • Integrate with formal verification tools
  • Create more diverse and challenging mathematical benchmarks
  • Address the subconscious plagiarism problem proactively

For Mathematical Researchers:

  • Consider AI as a tool for exploration, not a replacement for mathematical insight
  • Be prepared for the challenge of verifying AI-generated solutions
  • Contribute to improving problem databases and knowledge organization
  • Develop expertise in AI-assisted mathematical research workflows
  • Maintain critical evaluation of AI-generated results

For Database Maintainers:

  • Improve searchability and indexing of mathematical problems
  • Develop better methods for tracking solution status
  • Consider AI-assisted verification of problem states
  • Acknowledge the obscurity problem in "open" problem listings

7.4 The Big Picture

This study represents an important step in understanding AI's capabilities and limitations in mathematical research. It demonstrates that:

1. AI has reached a thresholdwhere it can contribute to mathematical discovery

2. Significant challenges remainin accuracy, reliability, and novelty detection

3. Human expertise remains essentialfor verifying and interpreting AI outputs

4. Systematic issues existin mathematical knowledge organization

5. Cautious optimism is warrantedrather than hype or dismissal

The study concludes by emphasizing that while these results provide an engaging new benchmark for AI researchers, they should not be overinterpreted as evidence that AI is ready to revolutionize mathematics. The low-hanging fruit has been reached, but the truly challenging problems await both human and artificial mathematicians.
